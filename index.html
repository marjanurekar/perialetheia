Perialetheia opens on the anniversary of the first publication of The Fellowship of the Ring (29 July 1954) - a coincidence this laboratory is content to make deliberate. Tolkien’s One Ring is, in the language of measurement, a corrupted reference: an object every bearer trusts and refers their judgement to, which silently distorts every judgement made against it. Its danger is precisely that it cannot be audited from within by anyone who holds it. The corruption is invisible to the instrument it has captured, and the ring is undone only from outside the system that trusts it. That is the failure this laboratory exists to catch. A reference that cannot be checked against something independent of itself will, when it is wrong, make every measurement founded on it wrong in the same direction and with the same confidence, and no amount of internal consistency will reveal it. Perialetheia’s discipline is to name its references, verify them against sources outside the claim, and declare what it cannot yet check.

Orientation · for readers new to the laboratory

What this laboratory is

Two ideas underlie everything here. One is a framework for treating belief as a measured quantity; the other is the laboratory that applies it. Neither requires prior familiarity with metrology or with information conflict.

CWIMF - the measurement framework

The Cognitive Warfare Information Metrology Framework treats a claim circulating in the information environment the way a laboratory treats any measurand. A proposition is anchored to a verified truth value; the prevalence of belief in it is measured against that anchor; and the result is reported with a full uncertainty budget in the sense of the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM). The central quantity is belief displacement: how far a population’s belief sits from what the verified evidence supports. Displacement is reported the way any measurement is, with a stated coverage interval and an explicit account of what has not been evaluated. The framework borrows its discipline directly from formal metrology - the International Vocabulary of Metrology (VIM), the GUM, and the laboratory-competence standard ISO/IEC 17025 - so that a figure about belief carries the same traceability a calibration certificate does.

CWIAL - the laboratory

Perialetheia is a Cognitive Warfare Information Analysis Laboratory: the first working instance (No. 1) that applies the framework to real cases. Where CWIMF is the method written down, CWIAL is the bench on which measurements are actually made - references verified, instruments calibrated, and reports issued clause by clause under an ISO/IEC 17025 structure. Each monthly report re-audits one contested figure from the public record and states what can, and what cannot yet, be measured from it.

Belief displacement
The signed distance between measured belief and the belief the verified evidence would support. The laboratory’s primary measurand.
Verified reference
A proposition with a stated truth value and a documented source chain. Every measurement is referred to one.
Uncertainty budget
The itemised account of every source of doubt in a figure, combined per GUM. No result is published without one.
Declared gaps
Quantities the record cannot yet support are stated as unmeasured - never omitted, never estimated.

The name Perialetheia joins peri‑ (around, concerning) with alētheia (truth, literally un‑concealment). The laboratory measures how belief stands in relation to what is verified, and treats every gap in that relation as something to be declared rather than hidden.

§7.8 - Reporting of results · Monthly report No. 2

The Brexit £350m claim

One displacement that can be measured, and one comparison, widely repeated, that cannot be made at all.

Report series
  1. No. 1 GM food safety beliefs in Europe amended · archive
  2. No. 2 The Brexit £350m claim current
Principal finding

The claim that belief in the £350m figure has hardly shifted since the referendum rests on setting 47% (2016) beside 42% (2018). The two readings do not share a base. The 2016 figure is the proportion of all GB adults who thought the claim true. The 2018 figure is the proportion of those who had heard it. Rebase either onto the other and the resulting interval contains the other wave’s reading. Neither stability nor decline can be established from the published record. The two waves also differ in mode, in age range, and in awareness, which fell eleven points between them.

Tested claim - proposition F
“The UK sends £350 million per week to the European Union.”
Verified true value V(F) = FALSE · VFRB tier 2
£350m is a gross figure taken before the UK rebate. For 2016/17 HM Treasury reports a gross contribution of £16.9bn, about £325m per week; £12.2bn after the £4.8bn rebate, about £235m per week; and £8.1bn net of public-sector receipts, about £156m per week. The UK Statistics Authority concluded on 21 April 2016 that the figure’s use was potentially misleading, and stated on 27 May 2016 that continued use of a gross figure in a context implying it was net was misleading and undermined trust in official statistics. The Authority issued no net estimate of its own, and none is attributed to it here.
Declared shortfall in the source chain
This laboratory requires at least three independent sources for a truth value. Two stand behind V(F): the UK Statistics Authority and HM Treasury. They are not fully independent, since the Authority is commenting on Treasury and ONS figures. The 2018 survey is not counted among them: it is the measuring instrument, and it is registered as one. The shortfall is stated rather than made up by counting the instrument as a reference.

Why the comparison does not close

Respondents in 2018 who had not heard the claim were not asked whether they believed it. What share of them would have endorsed it on being shown it is unmeasured, and it is the whole of the gap between the two bases. Bounding that share at nothing and at everything gives the widest interval the record permits, and that interval is wider than the change under discussion.

Archive · Monthly report No. 1 · amended 25 August 2026

GM food safety beliefs in Europe

A measurement error in the public record, and one anchor measurement that survives it.

Amendment · supersedes the reading published 29 July 2026

Base. The anchor item in EB 73.1 was administered on a split ballot, so its base is the half-sample of approximately 15,600, not the full wave of 31,238. The Type A uncertainty on the anchor is accordingly 0.005, not the 0.004 first published. The displacement itself is unchanged at +0.59. The original figure is recorded here rather than removed. The n column in the source record reports wave size; for EB 73.1 the item base is the split-ballot half-sample.

Wave attribution. The 2005 concern rating is Special Eurobarometer 238 (wave EB 64.1), not EB 64.3. The biotechnology-series wave EB 64.3 is a separate citation and was previously conflated with it. The 2010 EFSA wave is EB 73.5.

Item wording. The anchor is disagreement with a positively worded safety statement, not agreement with a negatively worded one. The distinction matters because acquiescence bias pushes the two in opposite directions, and the earlier description had it the wrong way round.

Budget. The Type B evaluation is complete and is now the on-page reading rather than a working annex.

Principal finding

The widely cited collapse in European concern about GM food - from 63% (2005) to 27% (2019) - spans an undocumented change of survey instrument. On the instrument used in 2005 and 2010, the readings were 63% and 66%: essentially unchanged. The 2019 and 2022 waves switched to a different question format that mechanically lowers every item on the list. No part of the apparent decline can be attributed to belief change until the two formats are harmonised. In metrological terms, an uncharacterised change in the measuring instrument was read as a change in the measurand.

Tested claim - proposition F
“Consuming approved GM food is dangerous to human health.”
Verified true value V(F) = FALSE · VFRB tier 1
The World Health Organization, the EFSA GMO Panel, and the US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine (2016 review) each conclude that approved GM foods on the market present no greater risk to human health than their conventional counterparts.

The only pair that is one instrument read twice

Bounding how fast this belief can move organically requires two readings of an instrument that did not change between them. The 2005 and 2010 concern ratings do not qualify: the list grew from 14 items to 17, the wording moved from products to organisms, and the population went from EU-25 to EU-27. A report that treats a change of referent as a change of instrument cannot treat three simultaneous changes as none.

One pair does qualify. The four-judgement battery of 1996 and 1999 - useful, a risk for society, morally acceptable, should be encouraged - was administered unchanged, and its risk for society item rose from 67% to 73% across 37 months of fieldwork. That is 1.95 percentage points a year, disclosed here as 2.0 to stay conservative. Two things must be said alongside it. The direction is upward, against the received narrative of declining concern. And the share expressing no view rose from 12% to 20% over the same interval, which inflates the agreeing share among those who answered.

What the rate bound yields

At most 2.0 pp/yr of organic movement over the nine years from 2010 to 2019 accounts for 18 of the 39-point gap between the 66% and 27% readings. At least 21 points - 54% of the gap - is instrument. This is a bound, not a decomposition: it is the least that can be attributed to the change of format, not the most.

The 1996 and 1999 marginals are extracted from the GESIS codebooks (ZA2899, ZA3205) and are not available from secondary sources. Anyone checking this result should go to the codebooks, and this laboratory would rather be corrected than believed.

One wave asks directly about health safety and therefore maps onto proposition F without a bridging assumption. It is the only wave from which a belief displacement can currently be computed.

§7.2 - Selection and validation of methods

How a measurement is made

Five stages, in order. Each one has to complete before the next is meaningful.

  1. Anchor the claimMatch the claim to a Verified Factual Reference Base entry: a proposition with a stated truth value, a documented source chain of at least three independent Grade I–II sources, and a re-verification date.
  2. Define the population and the instrumentState exactly who is being measured and with what. A survey question format is an instrument; changing it changes the reading. This is where most published trend claims fail.
  3. Compute the displacementBelief prevalence minus the prevalence that would align with the verified truth. For a verified-false proposition this is the prevalence itself.
  4. Build the uncertainty budgetType A from the sampling. Type B for reference classification risk, instrument bias, temporal mismatch, representativeness, and adversarial adaptation. Combine per GUM.
  5. Report, including the gapsAn ISO/IEC 17025 clause 7.8 report: measurand, reference, method, budget, result with coverage factor, detection capability, and an explicit statement of what was not evaluated.
§7.2.1 - Traceability to published standards

The standards this laboratory works to

Every term used here maps onto an established measurement standard. Nothing in the method is invented where an accepted definition already exists.

JCGM 200:2012 VIM - International Vocabulary of Metrology

Supplies the core concepts: measurand, reference, calibration, traceability. A belief measurement is defined against a measurand and a reference exactly as any physical measurement is.

JCGM 100:2008 GUM - Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement

Supplies the uncertainty budget: Type A and Type B components, combination in quadrature, coverage factor and expanded uncertainty. Every reported figure carries a budget built this way.

ISO/IEC 17025:2017 Competence of testing and calibration laboratories

Supplies the reporting discipline. Reports follow the clause 7.8 structure: measurand, method, budget, result with coverage factor, and an explicit statement of what was not evaluated.

Currie 1968 · ISO 11843-1:1997 Capability of detection

Supplies the decision rule: the critical value a displacement must exceed to be distinguished from zero, and the minimum detectable displacement of the instrument. Both characterise what the instrument can resolve rather than what a population believes. Where the null state cannot be physically prepared - no population has ever held a false belief at zero prevalence - this laboratory reports them as capability statements, and rests its substantive claims on differences, where a null of no change is attainable.

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 AI management systems

Governs the AI components the laboratory uses as instruments - the language-model classifiers that score narrative consistency and screen sources. It requires that each be validated before use, operated with documented uncertainty, and monitored for bias and drift, so that a figure produced with the help of a model carries the same accountability as one from any other instrument.

Where this laboratory departs from a standard, it says so and why. Where it follows one, the mapping above is the audit trail.
